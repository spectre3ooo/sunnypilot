# Learned values per speed band — Ioniq 5

Generated by `openpilot/tools/lat_metrics/learner_bands.py` (T-1) over 947 segments
(31 rlog, 916 qlog; 25 routes, 2026-09-03 to 2026-10). Full output, including JSON:
`logs/learner_bands/` in the experiments directory.

This is the offline fit that `torqued-lowspeed.md` says should come before any online
work. It answers that note's question — *does K(v) vary enough below 15 m/s to matter?*
— with **yes**, by about a factor of two.

## Method, briefly

- **Torque:** the upstream `TorqueEstimator`, replayed with only `MIN_VEL` lowered to
  2 m/s. Each accepted point is tagged with vEgo and fitted per band. Fits are balanced
  the way torqued's full buckets are: at most 1500 points per steer bucket, and nothing
  with |steer| ≥ 0.5.
- **Lag:** one upstream `LateralLagEstimator` per band. Each one's floor is lagd's own
  `min_vego` gate, and samples above the band's ceiling are masked out.
- **Steer ratio:** this is not a Kalman-filter replay. It regresses steady-state measured
  curvature on `VehicleModel.curvature_factor(v) × angle` per band, using the logged
  stiffness, roll and offset.
- **Control check:** every learner also gets a *stock gates* row that uses the on-device
  gates. The torque stock row reproduces the car's learned value (2.80, against 2.77 on
  recent routes), and steer ratio comes within 2 %. Confidence intervals bootstrap over
  segments, not samples.

## Torque response: strongly speed dependent

Median lateral accel per steer bucket (|lat acc| ≤ 3 m/s²):

| band | −0.5…−0.3 | −0.3…−0.2 | −0.2…−0.1 | −0.1…0 | 0…0.1 | 0.1…0.2 | 0.2…0.3 | 0.3…0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| < 15 mph | −0.75 | −0.62 | −0.48 | −0.38 | −0.29 | 0.00 | 0.12 | 0.16 |
| 15–25 mph | −1.03 | −0.73 | −0.55 | −0.38 | −0.26 | −0.11 | −0.00 | 0.24 |
| 25–40 mph | −1.23 | −0.89 | −0.66 | −0.45 | −0.23 | −0.01 | 0.22 | 0.48 |
| > 40 mph | −1.28 | −0.95 | −0.67 | −0.40 | −0.15 | 0.11 | 0.33 | 0.71 |

Fitting a line through those medians gives this slope (|lat acc| ≤ 1, 90 % CI):

| band | median slope | 90 % CI | friction (torqued's spread × 1.5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 15 mph | **1.34** | 1.10–1.64 | 0.153 |
| 15–25 mph | **1.43** | 1.29–1.53 | 0.145 |
| 25–40 mph | **1.95** | 1.89–2.02 | 0.138 |
| > 40 mph | **2.46** | 2.40–2.66 | 0.094 |
| stock gates | 2.40 | 2.35–2.46 | 0.101 |

**The same steering torque produces roughly half the lateral acceleration below 25 mph
that it does above 40.** The controller runs on one learned factor (about 2.8, learned
above 34 mph) at every speed. So at low speed `torque_from_lateral_accel` asks for about
half the torque the car actually needs, and the PID has to make up the rest. That is
consistent with what `baseline.md` measures: tracking error grows as speed falls, and it
is largest right after reversals, where a feedforward shortfall can't be integrated away
in time. It also explains why low-speed KP in `latcontrol_torque.py` is so high.

### Why there are two slopes

torqued's own fit is total least squares (TLS), which assumes equal noise on both axes.
When the lateral-accel spread is large next to the signal, as it is at low speed, TLS
rotates the line steep. Run per band, it reports the *opposite* trend: 3.19 below 15 mph
against 2.87 above 40. `test_learner_bands.py` pins this with synthetic data. So compare
bands on the median slope, and use TLS only for the like-for-like check against the car.
The absolute values differ between the two estimators (2.40 vs 2.80 at stock gates); the
ratio between bands is the result.

### Caveats

- The low bands are mostly corner entries and exits, so they hold more transient data
  than the highway bands. torqued shifts points by the learned lag, but transients still
  flatten a slope. The raw curve shows the effect plainly, but its exact size is softer
  than the CI suggests.
- The positive-steer side at < 15 mph flattens out (0.12 → 0.16). That could be a real
  asymmetry or a selection effect, since few points are left there.
- The data mixes NNLC-off and NNLC-on drives. This shouldn't matter, because torqued
  learns the plant (commanded torque → lateral accel), not the controller. It is still
  worth a split once `LateralControlSP.nnlcActive` is in enough logs.
- Friction is about 50 % higher below 40 mph. Some of that is real EPS friction and
  hysteresis, and some is the extra transient content in those bands.

## Steer ratio: flat with speed

| band | steerRatio | 90 % CI |
|---|---|---|
| < 15 mph | 15.88 | 15.80–15.97 |
| 15–25 mph | 15.79 | 15.69–15.90 |
| 25–40 mph | 15.59 | 15.52–15.66 |
| > 40 mph | 15.82 | 15.72–15.91 |
| stock gates | 15.73 | 15.68–15.78 (logged 15.45) |

Speed moves SR by under 2 %. The angle split moves it more, about 16.3 at 2–10° down to
15.6 at 20–45°, and does so consistently in every speed band. That is either a mild
variable-ratio rack or a small-angle offset bias. Either way it is **not a speed effect,
so per-band SR isn't worth building.** An earlier rough check that suggested SR fell
below 25 mph came from a 30-minute sample and is withdrawn.

## Lateral delay: cannot be answered from these logs

lagd needs at least 25 s of okay samples inside a 60 s window: engaged, no driver
torque, not saturated, sensors valid, and a 2 s recovery after each interruption. Across
the 31 rlogs, the stock > 50 mph gate saw **4 s** of qualifying driving, and no band
produced a usable estimate. qlogs can't substitute: their deviceMotion runs at about
5 Hz, which is too coarse to cross-correlate a 0.2 s lag.

To answer this, collect more rlogs, ideally fixed routes covering each band. Relaxing
lagd's window for the offline run would also work, but then it is no longer lagd's
estimator.

## What this means for the open questions

- **Per-band torque is worth pursuing.** Here it is the biggest single gap between what
  the controller assumes and what the car does at low speed. Before any online learner,
  a cheaper first experiment is a **fixed speed schedule** on the learned latAccelFactor
  (a scale of about 0.55 at < 25 mph, rising to 1.0 by about 40 mph), A/B-tested with
  the alternating-arm protocol.
- **NNLC takes vEgo as an input, so it may already model this.** That would explain why
  NNLC improved the sub-15 mph second-bend peak by 63 % in `baseline.md`, and it bears on
  whether NNLC stays on. The next comparison: NNLC's implied torque → lat-accel gain per
  band against the median slopes above.
- **SR per band:** no. **Lag per band:** unknown until more rlogs exist.
