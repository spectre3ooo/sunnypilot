# Learned values per speed band — Ioniq 5

Generated by `openpilot/tools/lat_metrics/learner_bands.py` (T-1) over 947 segments
(31 rlog, 916 qlog; 25 routes, 2026-09-03 to 2026-10). Full output, including JSON:
`logs/learner_bands/` in the experiments directory.

This is the offline fit that `torqued-lowspeed.md` says should come before any online
work. It answers that note's question — *does K(v) vary enough below 15 m/s to matter?*
— with **yes**, by about a factor of two.

## Method, briefly

- **Torque:** the upstream `TorqueEstimator`, replayed with only `MIN_VEL` lowered to
  2 m/s. Each accepted point is tagged with vEgo and fitted per band. Fits are balanced
  the way torqued's full buckets are: at most 1500 points per steer bucket, and nothing
  with |steer| ≥ 0.5.
- **Lag:** one upstream `LateralLagEstimator` per band. Each one's floor is lagd's own
  `min_vego` gate, and samples above the band's ceiling are masked out.
- **Steer ratio:** this is not a Kalman-filter replay. It regresses steady-state measured
  curvature on `VehicleModel.curvature_factor(v) × angle` per band, using the logged
  stiffness, roll and offset.
- **Control check:** every learner also gets a *stock gates* row that uses the on-device
  gates. The torque stock row reproduces the car's learned value (2.80, against 2.77 on
  recent routes), and steer ratio comes within 2 %. Confidence intervals bootstrap over
  segments, not samples.

## Torque response: strongly speed dependent

Median lateral accel per steer bucket (|lat acc| ≤ 3 m/s²):

| band | −0.5…−0.3 | −0.3…−0.2 | −0.2…−0.1 | −0.1…0 | 0…0.1 | 0.1…0.2 | 0.2…0.3 | 0.3…0.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| < 15 mph | −0.75 | −0.62 | −0.48 | −0.38 | −0.29 | 0.00 | 0.12 | 0.16 |
| 15–25 mph | −1.03 | −0.73 | −0.55 | −0.38 | −0.26 | −0.11 | −0.00 | 0.24 |
| 25–40 mph | −1.23 | −0.89 | −0.66 | −0.45 | −0.23 | −0.01 | 0.22 | 0.48 |
| > 40 mph | −1.28 | −0.95 | −0.67 | −0.40 | −0.15 | 0.11 | 0.33 | 0.71 |

Fitting a line through those medians gives this slope (|lat acc| ≤ 1, 90 % CI):

| band | median slope | 90 % CI | friction (torqued's spread × 1.5) |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 15 mph | **1.34** | 1.10–1.64 | 0.153 |
| 15–25 mph | **1.43** | 1.29–1.53 | 0.145 |
| 25–40 mph | **1.95** | 1.89–2.02 | 0.138 |
| > 40 mph | **2.46** | 2.40–2.66 | 0.094 |
| stock gates | 2.40 | 2.35–2.46 | 0.101 |

**The same steering torque produces roughly half the lateral acceleration below 25 mph
that it does above 40.** The controller runs on one learned factor (about 2.8, learned
above 34 mph) at every speed. So at low speed `torque_from_lateral_accel` asks for about
half the torque the car actually needs, and the PID has to make up the rest. That is
consistent with what `baseline.md` measures: tracking error grows as speed falls, and it
is largest right after reversals, where a feedforward shortfall can't be integrated away
in time. It also explains why low-speed KP in `latcontrol_torque.py` is so high.

### Why there are two slopes

torqued's own fit is total least squares (TLS), which assumes equal noise on both axes.
When the lateral-accel spread is large next to the signal, as it is at low speed, TLS
rotates the line steep. Run per band, it reports the *opposite* trend: 3.19 below 15 mph
against 2.87 above 40. `test_learner_bands.py` pins this with synthetic data. So compare
bands on the median slope, and use TLS only for the like-for-like check against the car.
The absolute values differ between the two estimators (2.40 vs 2.80 at stock gates); the
ratio between bands is the result.

### Caveats

- The low bands are mostly corner entries and exits, so they hold more transient data
  than the highway bands. torqued shifts points by the learned lag, but transients still
  flatten a slope. The raw curve shows the effect plainly, but its exact size is softer
  than the CI suggests.
- The positive-steer side at < 15 mph flattens out (0.12 → 0.16). That could be a real
  asymmetry or a selection effect, since few points are left there.
- The data mixes NNLC-off and NNLC-on drives. This shouldn't matter, because torqued
  learns the plant (commanded torque → lateral accel), not the controller. It is still
  worth a split once `LateralControlSP.nnlcActive` is in enough logs.
- Friction is about 50 % higher below 40 mph. Some of that is real EPS friction and
  hysteresis, and some is the extra transient content in those bands.

## Steer ratio: flat with speed

| band | steerRatio | 90 % CI |
|---|---|---|
| < 15 mph | 15.88 | 15.80–15.97 |
| 15–25 mph | 15.79 | 15.69–15.90 |
| 25–40 mph | 15.59 | 15.52–15.66 |
| > 40 mph | 15.82 | 15.72–15.91 |
| stock gates | 15.73 | 15.68–15.78 (logged 15.45) |

Speed moves SR by under 2 %. The angle split moves it more, about 16.3 at 2–10° down to
15.6 at 20–45°, and does so consistently in every speed band. That is either a mild
variable-ratio rack or a small-angle offset bias. Either way it is **not a speed effect,
so per-band SR isn't worth building.** An earlier rough check that suggested SR fell
below 25 mph came from a 30-minute sample and is withdrawn.

## Lateral delay: needs rlogs from the right driving

lagd needs at least 25 s of okay samples inside a 60 s window: engaged, no driver
torque, not saturated, sensors valid, and a 2 s recovery after each interruption. qlogs
can't be used for the estimate itself: their deviceMotion runs at about 5 Hz, which is
too coarse to cross-correlate a 0.2 s lag. They can show *where* qualifying driving is,
though. `learner_bands.py --screen-lag` approximates lagd's okay test from a qlog's
controls messages, and on the 31 rlogs it matches the real lagd replay to within a few
seconds per band (4/73/159/556/50 s vs 4/70/159/554/49 s).

- **The original 31 rlogs** were picked for low-speed corner work. The stock > 50 mph gate
  saw 4 s of qualifying driving in them, and no band produced a stock-window estimate.
- **The old qlogs** (947 segments) hold about 13,000 s of qualifying driving above 50 mph,
  but under 2,000 s in each band below 25 mph.
- **The device's 867 current segments** (screened on the car,
  `logs/device_lag_screen.csv`) hold 10,300 s above 50 mph, but only 444 s below 15 mph
  and 750 s at 15–25 mph. **Low-speed driving almost never gives lagd 25 clean seconds
  in a minute**, so stock lagd can't estimate low-speed lag from any amount of this
  driving.
- `--lag-okay-window 10` relaxes the window. It's flagged non-stock in the report,
  because it is no longer lagd's own estimator. On the 31 rlogs it gives a first hint:
  25–40 mph median 0.29 s (5 segments) and 15–25 mph 0.36 s (2 segments), against
  0.21 s learned on the highway. **Lag may grow as speed falls, but this is too little
  data to act on.** The 100 device segments with the most low- and mid-speed qualifying
  time are listed in `logs/device_rlog_picks.txt`.

## NNLC: models the trend, not the size

`openpilot/tools/lat_metrics/nnlc_gain.py` evaluates the `HYUNDAI_IONIQ_5` NNLC model
(fleet-trained, 2023-08) in steady state. With `--logs`, it also checks the model against
driving. NNLC state isn't logged on official builds, so each segment is classified from
`torqueState.error`: the stock controller logs exactly desired − actual there (median
mismatch ~1e-8), while NNLC overwrites it (≥ 1e-2). The split is clean and agrees with
`baseline.md`'s inferred on/off routes: 81 segments on, 505 off.

Steady-state sweep over ±1 m/s², flat road:

| speed | NNLC latAccelFactor | measured median slope | NNLC / measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 mph | 3.26 | 1.34 | 2.4 |
| 20 mph | 3.45 | 1.43 | 2.4 |
| 32.5 mph | 3.73 | 1.95 | 1.9 |
| 55 mph | 4.31 | 2.46 | 1.75 |

Against driving: in steady, tracking frames (\|desired jerk\| < 0.3, \|desired −
actual\| < 0.2), take the slope of gravity-adjusted lat accel against the torque
actually commanded. That is a torqued-free estimate of what the car does. The same slope
against NNLC's feedforward for those frames is what the model believes:

| NNLC-off segments | plant gain | 90 % CI | NNLC gain | plant / NNLC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15–25 mph | 2.04 | 1.90–2.17 | 3.67 | 0.55 |
| 25–40 mph | 2.47 | 2.40–2.54 | 3.85 | 0.64 |
| > 40 mph | 2.49 | 2.45–2.54 | 4.02 | 0.62 |

The plant gain independently confirms the torque result: it rises with speed, and on
the highway it lands on torqued's value. (Below 15 mph, the commanded torque is mostly
feedback and the lat-accel range is tiny, which drags a regression slope towards zero.
That row keeps only its direction. A ratio-of-levels check agrees: there, commanded
torque is about 1.8–2× NNLC's feedforward.)

**So the NNLC model believes this car gives about 1.6× more lateral accel per unit torque
than it does on the highway, and about 2× more at 15–25 mph.** Its implied gain does
fall with speed, but only about half as much as the car's does. NNLC's feedforward
therefore under-asks at every speed, and more so at low speed, which is the same shape
of error as the stock controller's single latAccelFactor. At 10 mph, NNLC actually asks
for *less* torque per m/s² than the stock controller (1/3.26 vs 1/2.8).

That changes how to read `baseline.md`'s NNLC result. NNLC's better sub-15 mph tracking
can't come from a better feedforward. The likely source is its feedback path: below
30 m/s, `nnlc.py` adds `LOW_SPEED_FACTOR`² × curvature to both setpoint and measurement
(up to 144× near standstill). That gives a much stiffer low-speed error response than the
stock PID.

Why the model is off by about 1.6× even on the highway isn't established. It was trained
in 2023 on fleet data. The training cars may have had a different EPS firmware (this
car's is `NE MDPS R 1.00 1.06 57700GI000 4NEDR106`), or the torque normalisation may have
changed since then. Either way, this car's own data says the model's absolute scale is
wrong for it.

## Update: 866 rlogs from the device (2026-10-16)

With the device on the bench, all 866 rlogs on it (routes 0x28–0x3f, official sunnypilot `dev`, V0
controller, NNLC off, stock limits) were pulled and the analysis rerun over 1,813 segments: 897 rlog, 916
qlog. Outputs: `logs/learner_bands_v2_lag25/` (stock lagd window) and `logs/learner_bands_v2_lag10/`.
The stock-gates rows now reproduce the car's logged values closely: latAccelFactor 2.63 vs 2.60 logged,
and lag 0.245 s vs 0.239 s.

**Torque**, as the median slope with 90 % CI, |lat acc| ≤ 1:

| band | median slope | 90 % CI | plant gain from commanded torque (NNLC-off) |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 15 mph | **0.91** | 0.65–1.28 | 0.67 (0.54–0.80) |
| 15–25 mph | **1.58** | 1.46–1.66 | 1.94 (1.85–2.02) |
| 25–40 mph | **1.98** | 1.92–2.04 | 2.38 (2.31–2.44) |
| > 40 mph | **2.38** | 2.35–2.43 | 2.40 (2.35–2.44) |

The low-speed drop is larger than the first pass showed: below 15 mph it is about 0.3–0.4× the highway
value. The opt-in latAccelFactor schedule (0.6 / 0.75 / 1.0) is therefore *conservative* at the low end.
Keep it for the first A/B, and go lower only if arm B is clearly better and doesn't run into the cap.
The rate schedule's jerk test assumed 1.64 m/s² at full torque below 10 mph; the new data says less, so
it has more margin than it was checked for.

**Steer ratio:** unchanged, flat with speed (15.65–15.97).

**Lateral delay: grows as speed falls.** Both windows agree:

| band | stock window (25 s) | relaxed window (10 s, non-stock) |
|---|---|---|
| > 40 mph | 0.246 s, 146 segs | 0.247 s (0.239–0.258), 173 segs |
| 25–40 mph | 0.300 s, 20 segs | 0.304 s (0.292–0.312), 50 segs |
| 15–25 mph | 0.370 s, 7 segs | 0.368 s (0.351–0.372), 17 segs |
| < 15 mph | 0.31 s, 1 seg | 0.31 s (0.26–0.35), 4 segs (too thin) |

The car uses ~0.24 s everywhere, but below 40 mph the delay from requested to achieved lateral accel is
0.30–0.37 s. **One caveat matters.** lagd measures the whole path from desired to actual lateral accel,
and below 25 mph the rate limit alters the request on ~80 % of engaged frames. Part of this apparent
low-speed delay is therefore the rate limit itself, which the new rate schedule reduces. Measure lag per
band again on A/B arm data before building a lag schedule, or the schedule will compensate for a limiter
that has since been loosened.

**NNLC:** with 904 NNLC-off segments, plant / NNLC gain is 0.17 / 0.53 / 0.62 / 0.63 from the lowest
band up. Same conclusion, tighter.

## What was changed (2026-10-16)

The limits question is answered the same way as the feedforward: **by speed, not overall**. The jerk a
torque rate can cause scales with lateral accel per unit torque, which roughly halves at low speed.

- **Flat 10/10 rate reverted** (opendbc 8a3b99b6). It exceeded opendbc's ISO 11270 jerk test at every
  speed (5.43 m/s³ against 3.0 up and 5.0 down), and had made that test skip CAN FD HKG. It was never driven.
- **Speed-scheduled torque rate up, Ioniq 5 only** (opendbc 23a60c0e restores the generic panda mechanism;
  dd7a76e0 enables it). The steps are 4 counts/frame below 20 mph, 3 up to ~27 mph, and the stock 2 above
  that. Rate down stays 3 and the cap stays 270. panda enforces the same table, gated by
  `HYUNDAI_PARAM_SP_CANFD_LOW_SPEED_RATE`, which is set only for `HYUNDAI_IONIQ_5`. Other CAN FD HKG cars
  are unchanged, because nobody has measured their response per speed. `test_scheduled_rate_jerk_limits`
  checks each speed against the measured lateral accel at full torque (upper 90 % CI): the max is 2.43 m/s³,
  under the 2.5 bound with no tolerance. **This is always on for the Ioniq 5 on this branch**, since a panda
  limit can't be toggled per drive.
- **latAccelFactor speed schedule, opt-in** (`LatAccelFactorSpeedSchedule`, default off). It scales the
  learned latAccelFactor by 0.6 at ≤ 10 mph, 0.75 at 20 mph and 1.0 from 34 mph, which sits between the two
  estimators above. It's applied in both torque controllers. The car runs `LatControlTorqueV0`, because
  `EnforceTorqueControl` is off, so the v1 file alone would have done nothing. It's skipped when NNLC drives
  the feedforward. It's logged per frame as `lateralControlSP.latAccelFactorScale` (1.0 when off, 0.0 in
  older logs).
- **Not changed:** the 270 cap. Projected with the corrected feedforward, it would bind on ~20 % of
  sub-15 mph frames. Raising it needs an EPS-acceptance and driver-overridability test first.

### Running the A/B

Flash this branch. The rate schedule is then always on, so every arm below shares it; compare against the
older logs for the rate's own effect. Alternate routes between:

| arm | `LatAccelFactorSpeedSchedule` | `LatExperimentArm` |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 10 |
| B | 1 | 11 |

The param is read when controls start, so change it between drives. Set `LatExperimentArm` to match, so the
arm is in every log; `latAccelFactorScale` confirms it independently. Keep NNLC off for both arms. Score
with `lat_metrics.py`: pooled p95 tracking error and the second-bend peak per band, plus intervention
share. `limitedCap` matters most, because if arm B mostly moves frames from rate-limited to cap-limited,
the cap is the next limit to work on.

## What this means for the open questions

- **Per-band torque is worth pursuing for both controllers.** For the stock controller:
  a **fixed speed schedule** on the learned latAccelFactor (a scale of about 0.55 below
  25 mph, rising to 1.0 by about 40 mph). For NNLC: a **speed-scheduled scale on its
  feedforward** (about 1.6× on the highway, about 1.8× at 15–25 mph, more below), or a
  model retrained on this car's data. A/B-test each with the alternating-arm protocol.
- **NNLC on or off** is then a comparison of corrected controllers, not of the shipped
  ones. Shipped NNLC wins at low speed through stiffer feedback, not a better plant model.
- **SR per band:** no. **Lag per band:** stock lagd can't measure it below 25 mph from
  normal driving. A relaxed window hints that lag grows at low speed; confirm with the
  device rlogs.
